# Part C — Inventorship, ownership, and the right to file

21 items (9 Fatal).

Grounded in 35 U.S.C. §§ 100, 111, 115–118, 171–161, 261–262; 37 CFR 1.41–1.46, 1.63, 1.76; MPEP 210, 300, 400, 1500, 1600.

## Naming the correct inventors

### `C01` Name every true inventor and only true inventors
- **Check:** Verify that the inventor list on the declaration/ADS contains every person who contributed an inventive concept and no one who did not.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Compare everyone who touched the project against the names on the papers. Courtesy names and quietly omitted contributors are both defects. Derive the list from the claims, not from the org chart.
- **Why it matters:** Incorrect inventorship can render a patent invalid if the error cannot be corrected, and an omitted contributor can later assert ownership.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 100(f), 115–116; 37 CFR 1.41, 1.48; MPEP 210, 602.01.

### `C02` Test each named inventor against the one-claim contribution standard
- **Check:** Confirm that every named inventor contributed at least one inventive concept that is actually recited in at least one claim of the draft.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially joint inventors
- **What to look for:** Point to the specific claim limitation each person conceived. A dependent claim is enough. Contributors need not have worked together, at the same time, or equally. A technician whose suggestion appears in a claim must be named. A name that maps to no limitation is wrong; a limitation that maps to no named person means someone is missing.
- **Why it matters:** Naming someone with no claimed contribution, or omitting someone with one, is incorrect inventorship.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 116; *Ethicon v. U.S. Surgical*; MPEP 2100 (inventorship).

### `C03` Strike builders, financiers, advisers, and honorary inventors
- **Check:** Confirm that no one is named merely for building/testing the invention, funding it, advising on business, or holding a title such as CTO or project lead.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially corporate and funded filings
- **What to look for:** Company conventions that put an executive on every filing. Hours of labor do not count. These people can take ownership by assignment; they cannot take inventorship by courtesy.
- **Why it matters:** Naming non-inventors is a false statement on a declaration and can invalidate the patent if done in bad faith.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 115–116; 37 CFR 1.63; MPEP 602.

### `C04` Re-verify inventorship against the final claim set and record each contribution
- **Check:** Confirm inventorship was re-reviewed after the last claim revision and that each inventor's specific contribution is written down and signed.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially joint inventors
- **What to look for:** An inventor list frozen before the last claim edit. If a person's only contribution was cancelled, that person no longer belongs. Keep a contemporaneous contribution statement; it is not filed with the Office.
- **Why it matters:** Stale inventorship tied to deleted claim matter is incorrect inventorship. Without records you cannot later prove who invented what.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 116; 37 CFR 1.48; MPEP 602.01(c).

### `C05` Confirm documentation exists to defeat a later omitted-inventor claim
- **Check:** Verify that lab notebooks and signed consultant/contractor agreements exist for everyone who worked on the invention but is not named.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially where outside model makers or consultants were used
- **What to look for:** Technical diaries signed and witnessed, plus written assignments from consultants covering anything they conceive. A project run on email and memory, with an outside builder and no agreement, is the danger sign. This must exist before filing.
- **Why it matters:** After filing, a dispute is answered only by whatever proof already exists.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 116, 261; state contract law on assignments; MPEP 301.

## Applicant and ownership

### `C06` Identify the applicant correctly and separately from the inventors
- **Check:** Confirm the papers name the correct applicant (inventor, assignee, or obligated assignee) and that the applicant designation is consistent across the ADS, declaration, and any assignment.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially assignee-filed applications
- **What to look for:** Post-AIA, an assignee or obligated assignee may apply (35 U.S.C. § 118). Mixed signals: an entity listed as applicant with no assignment or obligation in the file, or inventors listed as applicants when employment already vests ownership elsewhere. A nonprovisional may claim priority to a provisional that is not commonly owned if they share a common inventor.
- **Why it matters:** A wrong applicant designation produces defective title and can contradict the assignment record.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 111, 118; 37 CFR 1.42–1.46; MPEP 605.

### `C07` Obtain a signed oath or declaration from every inventor
- **Check:** Verify that each named inventor has personally executed an oath or declaration, even where a company is the applicant.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially assignee-filed applications
- **What to look for:** One executed declaration per inventor, names matching the ADS. An assignee's ability to file does not excuse inventor signatures. Flag missing inventors who have left, are unreachable, or refuse to sign before filing, and use the substitute-statement path only when 35 U.S.C. § 115 actually allows it.
- **Why it matters:** An application without each inventor's executed oath or a permitted substitute is formally defective.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 115; 37 CFR 1.63, 1.64; MPEP 602.

## Employed inventors

### `C08` Test the invention against the inventor's employment agreement
- **Check:** Obtain each inventor's employment agreement and determine whether its assignment clause already captures this invention.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** employed inventors
- **What to look for:** Typical triggers: made during employment; related to the employer's business; using employer time, facilities, or materials; within the scope of duties. "I invented it at home on a weekend" does not defeat a relatedness or scope-of-duties clause. Check whether the governing state limits how broad the clause may be.
- **Why it matters:** If the agreement already captures the invention, an individual filing in their own name is filing property they do not own.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 261; state employment and labor statutes; *Board of Trustees of Leland Stanford v. Roche*.

### `C09` Confirm the prior-inventions carve-out covers a pre-employment invention
- **Check:** If the inventor claims the invention predates the employment, verify it was disclosed on the prior-inventions list attached to the employment agreement.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** employed inventors
- **What to look for:** The schedule of pre-existing inventions attached to the signed agreement. An unlisted "pre-existing" invention is the classic dispute. Corroborate the claimed conception date against dated records, not recollection.
- **Why it matters:** An unlisted pre-employment invention is often treated as captured by the assignment clause.
- **Primary source:** the executed employment agreement; 35 U.S.C. § 261.

### `C10` Where there is no employment agreement, test employed-to-invent and shop rights
- **Check:** With no written EA, determine whether the inventor was hired or directed to make this invention, and whether company time, facilities, or materials were used.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** employed inventors with no written agreement
- **What to look for:** Hired-to-invent can vest equitable title in the employer even without a written assignment. If the employee was not hired to invent, they may own the patent subject to a nonexclusive, nontransferable shop right if employer resources were used. Reconstruct job description, assigned projects, and whose lab and hours were used.
- **Why it matters:** Employed-to-invent can defeat individual title. A shop right permanently encumbers exclusivity.
- **Primary source:** *United States v. Dubilier Condenser*; state common law; 35 U.S.C. § 261.

### `C11` Disclose gray-area inventions to the employer and obtain a written release before filing
- **Check:** Where the invention is within or near the scope of the employment agreement, verify the inventor disclosed it and holds a signed release before filing in their own name.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** employed inventors
- **What to look for:** Most agreements require disclosure of all inventions so the employer can decide. Silence is not permission. If the employer passed, get a written release and read whether a shop right was reserved.
- **Why it matters:** Filing on an undisclosed gray-area invention invites an ownership claim and a breach-of-agreement dispute.
- **Primary source:** the employment agreement; 35 U.S.C. § 261.

## Joint inventors and co-owners

### `C12` Put a joint-owners agreement in place before filing
- **Check:** Where there will be two or more owners of the application, verify a signed joint-owners agreement (or equivalent) exists stating shares, consent, and profit sharing.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** joint inventors and multi-owner applications
- **What to look for:** Absent agreement, 35 U.S.C. § 262 lets any co-owner make, use, sell, or license without accounting to the others. Look for a signed agreement on percentages, consent to license or assign, and profit sharing. Do not file it with the Office.
- **Why it matters:** Without it, any co-owner can undercut the others and keep the money.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 262; MPEP 301.

## Assignment and recording

### `C13` Verify an executed assignment for every transfer of ownership
- **Check:** Where ownership is meant to sit anywhere other than with the inventors, confirm a signed assignment document exists with all particulars filled in.
- **Severity:** Fatal (where an assignee is the applicant or owner of record)
- **Applies to:** all applications with an assignee, employed inventors, funded inventors
- **What to look for:** Named inventors own unless they have assigned. An employment agreement is not always a present assignment of this invention — read the verbs (*Stanford v. Roche*). Check assignor and assignee names, the interest assigned, the invention identification, and signatures. Get full consideration at execution; do not structure the transfer as a stream of future payments if title must be clean now.
- **Why it matters:** Without an executed assignment the intended owner has no title and the applicant designation is unsupported.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 261; 37 CFR 3.11–3.81; MPEP 301–302.

### `C14` Prepare the assignment for prompt recording with the PTO
- **Check:** Confirm the assignment and its recordation cover sheet are ready to record, with the execution date of the application filled in if recording at filing.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications with an assignee
- **What to look for:** Recording is a race: a later bona fide purchaser who records first can defeat an earlier unrecorded assignment (35 U.S.C. § 261). Plan to record promptly. If recording with the application, the serial number may be blank but execution date must be filled in. Use PTO-1595 and the current recordation fee.
- **Why it matters:** An unrecorded assignment can be defeated, leaving the intended owner with no title.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 261; 37 CFR Part 3; MPEP 302.

## Choosing the right patent type

### `C15` Confirm utility versus design is the right filing
- **Check:** Verify that what is novel about the invention matches the patent type being sought — function to utility, purely ornamental appearance to design.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially inventions with a distinctive shape
- **What to look for:** Ask whether the novel features were added to make the device work better or look better, and whether removing them would impair function. Functional shape belongs in a utility application even if it is also attractive. Where both function and ornament exist, consider two applications.
- **Why it matters:** The wrong type cannot protect what is actually inventive. The filing date and fee are spent on a right the Office will not grant.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 101, 171; MPEP 1502, 1504.01.

### `C16` Screen a design application against the design statutory bars and format
- **Check:** For a design filing, verify the claimed design is not functional, not surface-only ornamentation disconnected from an article, and not a natural shape — and that the papers are drawings plus a single formal claim.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** design applications
- **What to look for:** Significant functional utility belongs in a utility application. The design must be for an article of manufacture. A design application is primarily drawings plus one claim to the article "as shown" (or "as shown and described"). Extra utility-style claims are a template error.
- **Why it matters:** Functional, natural, or misformatted design claims will not be granted.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 171; 37 CFR 1.152–1.154; MPEP 1502–1504.

### `C17` Confirm plant-patent eligibility and reproduction mode
- **Check:** For a plant invention, verify the plant is asexually reproducible and that a plant patent, not a utility patent or PVPA protection, is the right route.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** plant applications
- **What to look for:** Plant patents (35 U.S.C. § 161) cover asexually reproduced plants, with statutory exclusions. Sexually reproduced varieties may belong under the Plant Variety Protection Act. A useful plant trait may also support a utility application. A plant application carries a single formal claim to the plant as shown and described.
- **Why it matters:** Filing the wrong plant vehicle seeks a right the Office or PVPO cannot grant.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 161–164; 7 U.S.C. §§ 2321 et seq. (PVPA); MPEP 1601–1610.

## Subject matter that cannot be patented

### `C18` Screen the claims against the judicial exceptions
- **Check:** Read each independent claim and confirm it does not recite a mathematical concept, a method of organizing human activity, a mental process, or another categorically excluded subject without significantly more.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially software, business-method, diagnostic, and data-processing inventions
- **What to look for:** Current USPTO groupings: mathematical concepts; certain methods of organizing human activity; mental processes. A claim is a mental process if it can be performed in the human mind even if a computer is intended. Also flag tax strategies used as a distinguishing feature and claims that read on a human organism.
- **Why it matters:** A claim set aimed at excluded subject matter cannot issue as drafted.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 101; AIA §§ 14, 33; MPEP 2106.

### `C19` Anchor software claims to a practical application
- **Check:** For a software-implemented invention, verify the claims are directed to a process or machine with a practical application rather than to a program as such.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** software and computer-implemented applications
- **What to look for:** Claims whose only substance is instructions on a generic processor. A passing draft describes the machine or transformation, or a technological improvement, and claims that application. Copyright or trade secret may be the better route for code as such.
- **Why it matters:** Computer programs per se are not a statutory class. Eligibility support must exist at filing.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 101; *Alice*; MPEP 2106.04–2106.05.

## Patent term, fees, and expectations

### `C20` Confirm the fee set and entity status match the filing
- **Check:** Verify the correct filing/search/examination fees for the patent type are budgeted at the right entity status, and that maintenance-fee obligations are understood.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Filing, search, and examination fees must all be paid. Confirm large / small / micro status is actually supportable (37 CFR 1.27, 1.29). Paper filing adds a surcharge. Utility patents carry maintenance fees; design and plant patents do not. Re-price against the current USPTO fee schedule — do not use stale tables.
- **Why it matters:** Underpayment delays examination. An unsupported entity claim is a certification defect. Unpaid maintenance fees later kill the patent.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 41; 37 CFR 1.16–1.21, 1.27, 1.29; current USPTO fee schedule.

### `C21` Check the applicant's expectations against what a patent actually does
- **Check:** Confirm the applicant understands the patent's term, that it confers no right to practice, and that it is not a defense against others' patents.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications, especially self-represented and first-time filers
- **What to look for:** Belief that a patent by itself produces money; that it authorizes making the product; that it shields the owner from others' patents. A patent is a right to exclude (35 U.S.C. § 154), not a right to practice. Utility term is generally 20 years from the earliest nonprovisional filing date claimed. Freedom to operate is a separate search.
- **Why it matters:** False expectations drive the wrong filing, skip clearance work, and misjudge the value of the right being sought.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. §§ 154, 271; MPEP 2701.
