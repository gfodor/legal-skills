# Part F — Specification — section-by-section drafting

29 items (3 Fatal).

Grounded in 35 U.S.C. § 112; 37 CFR 1.71–1.84; MPEP 608, 2161–2173.

## Sketches and preliminary drawings

### `F01` Provide a complete set of figures covering every claimed feature
- **Check:** Verify the draft's figures show every feature the claims recite or will recite, in views that communicate the structure, with any prior-art figure labeled and the drawings kept as a document separate from the specification text.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Tick each recited element against a figure. Prefer perspective views over a stack of unexplained elevations. Label prior-art figures. File drawings separately from the written description.
- **Why it matters:** Features shown nowhere cannot support claim amendments. Unlabeled prior-art figures become admissions. Drawings bound into the specification are a formal defect.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.83–1.84; MPEP 608.02.

### `F02` Enforce reference-numeral and labeling hygiene
- **Check:** Verify each unique part has its own numeral, the same part carries the same numeral in every figure, numerals are even where a spare series is used, and no numeral designates a whole figure.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** One numeral for two parts; two numerals for one part; numerals that collide with figure numbers; arrowheads on ordinary lead lines. Leave unused numbers for later insertions.
- **Why it matters:** Inconsistent numerals produce drawing objections and internal contradictions a court can read as indefiniteness.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.84(p); MPEP 608.02(e).

### `F03` Supply an enabling flowchart or program listing for software and process inventions
- **Check:** Verify that any software, computer-implemented, chemical, or mechanical process disclosure includes a flowchart or listing plus a written explanation detailed enough that a skilled practitioner needs only routine skill to implement it.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** software/business method; chemical, mechanical, and computer processes
- **What to look for:** A flowchart with no block-by-block explanation. Missing language, processor, and I/O description. Process inventions need a flowchart even when the process is fully described in words.
- **Why it matters:** A block diagram without an implementing explanation is non-enabling and, after filing, unfixable.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a); 37 CFR 1.83, 1.96; MPEP 2161.01.

## Opening sections

### `F04` Test the title for both over-narrowness and emptiness
- **Check:** Verify the title captures the essence of the invention, is 500 characters or fewer, and is neither narrower than the full scope of the disclosed embodiments nor so broad as to be meaningless.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A title that excludes an embodiment, or a title that names only a field ("Electrical apparatus"). Match the generality of the broadest independent claim.
- **Why it matters:** A narrow title can be used to argue a narrow reading. An empty title draws an objection and hurts searchability.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.72(a); MPEP 606.

### `F05` Verify the cross-reference paragraph and the limits of incorporation by reference
- **Check:** Verify that every provisional, parent, or technically related application the draft relies on is recited in the Cross-Reference section in benefit-claim form, and that no matter essential to enablement or claim support is left outside the application and merely incorporated by reference.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A missing provisional reference. Essential subject matter that exists only in an incorporated document — foreign offices may reject the incorporation entirely. Reproduce anything needed to make, use, or support a claim.
- **Why it matters:** An omitted benefit claim forfeits the earlier date. Incorporation is not a substitute for an enabling disclosure.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.57, 1.78; MPEP 608.01(p), 211.02.

### `F06` Confirm the Technical Field describes the field, not the invention
- **Check:** Verify the Technical Field section states a preexisting field at two levels of generality and does not describe the invention itself.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Noun phrases that only exist because of this invention. A passing section would be equally true of the prior art.
- **Why it matters:** Describing the invention here can be read as an admission that it is preexisting technology.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.77; MPEP 608.01(c).

## Background — prior art

### `F07` Confirm the Background never describes the invention or its advantages
- **Check:** Verify the Background/Prior Art section contains no description of the applicant's invention and no statement of its advantages.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Sentences that slide from "prior devices did X" into "the present approach does Y." The Background stops at the problem and the deficiencies of prior solutions.
- **Why it matters:** Anything describing the invention in the Background can be treated as admitted prior art.
- **Primary source:** MPEP 608.01(c), 2129.

### `F08` Confirm each prior-art reference is fully cited and factually distinguished
- **Check:** Verify every prior-art approach discussed is identified with inventor/author, number or publication, and date, and is criticized in factual — not opinionated — terms.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A named reference with no stated drawback. Derogatory adjectives without facts. Citations that will not transfer cleanly onto an IDS.
- **Why it matters:** Uncriticized prior art shrinks the apparent advance. Incomplete citations frustrate examination and the later IDS.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.56, 1.98; MPEP 608.01(c), 609.

### `F09` Scrub the Background for admissions and excess detail
- **Check:** Verify the Background does not admit that the problem was already recognized, does not describe the prior-art device in enough detail to isolate the applicant's changes, and stays short.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** "Long recognized" problem statements that give away credit for spotting the problem. A detailed prior-art teardown followed by "the improvement changes A to B," which isolates the delta for an obviousness attack. A few paragraphs that orient the reader are enough.
- **Why it matters:** Admitted recognition of the problem supports obviousness. An isolated delta lets a single reference showing just that change invalidate the patent.
- **Primary source:** MPEP 2141.02, 2129; *KSR*.

## Advantages and Summary

### `F10` Confirm advantages are attributed to embodiments and each is actually delivered
- **Check:** Verify the Advantages narrative avoids "the invention" and "objects," attributes benefits to one or more embodiments, and lists no advantage that is not fulfilled by at least one disclosed aspect.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** "An object of the invention is…" Cross-check each listed advantage against the description. Place any advantages paragraph at the start of the detailed description if used.
- **Why it matters:** Objects of "the invention" have been used to limit claims. An undelivered advantage is an inconsistency an adversary can exploit.
- **Primary source:** *Phillips*; MPEP 608.01(d).

### `F11` Confirm the Summary paraphrases every claim and states no object of the invention
- **Check:** Verify the Summary gives a broad overview plus text paraphrasing each of the claims, does not focus on a single embodiment, and never recites an "object of the invention."
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A summary written before the claims froze, or one that mirrors only claim 1. Rule 73 permits an object statement; practice is to avoid it.
- **Why it matters:** A summary keyed to one embodiment or reciting objects gives courts a basis to limit the claims.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.73; MPEP 608.01(d).

## Drawing description sections

### `F12` Confirm the Brief Description of Drawings is one short sentence per figure with no numerals
- **Check:** Verify each figure has its own short descriptive sentence and that the section contains no reference numerals, part-level detail, or operational description.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Sentence count matching figure count. Digits attached to parts. Creeping operational detail.
- **Why it matters:** A figure with no listed description draws an objection. Detail imported here can be read as characterizing every embodiment.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.74; MPEP 608.01(f).

### `F13` Confirm a List of Reference Numerals exists and reconciles both ways
- **Check:** Verify a separately headed list of reference numerals and part names is present and that every numeral on the drawings appears in the specification and every numeral in the specification appears on the drawings.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Absence of the list. Orphans in either direction. One numeral for two parts, or two names for one part. Put the list at the end of the detailed description so it does not interrupt the narrative.
- **Why it matters:** Orphan numerals are drawing/description inconsistencies and, in litigation, ambiguity about what a claim term refers to.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.84(p); MPEP 608.01(g), 608.02(e).

## Detailed Description

### `F14` Confirm static structure is described separately from operation, general before specific
- **Check:** Verify the Detailed Description describes the static physical structure of each embodiment in its own section, before and separate from the section describing how it operates, and moves from overall parts to details.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Structure and function run together so parts get skipped. A structural section that starts with a subcomponent. Verbs that say what things do before saying what they are.
- **Why it matters:** Mixing structure and operation is how enablement gaps appear.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a); MPEP 608.01(g).

### `F15` Confirm every part, every interconnection, and every claim term is described
- **Check:** Verify each part shown in the drawings is described, each part-to-part connection or mounting is expressly stated, and every term that appears in the claims appears first in the specification.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Adjacent parts with no sentence saying how they join. Claim nouns with no specification antecedent. Nothing should float unattached.
- **Why it matters:** Undescribed connections fail make-and-use. A claim term with no specification antecedent draws a § 112 rejection.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a)–(b); 37 CFR 1.75(d)(1); MPEP 608.01(o), 2173.05(e).

### `F16` Confirm consistent part naming and correct use of "a" and "the"
- **Check:** Verify each part is introduced once with alternative equivalent names and an indefinite article, then referred to by a single consistent name with the definite article thereafter.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A part called a "strut" in one paragraph and a "support" in the next, unless both names were given together at first mention. Introducing a part with "the" before it exists, or re-introducing it with "a."
- **Why it matters:** Shifting nomenclature creates antecedent-basis and construction problems that surface in the claims.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(b); MPEP 2173.05(e).

### `F17` Confirm nothing has been held back: dimensions, materials, relationships, and sources
- **Check:** Verify that every exotic, special, or critical part is described with its dimensions, exemplary materials, relationships, and sources of supply, with nothing withheld as a trade secret.
- **Severity:** Fatal
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** "A suitable adhesive," "an appropriate coating," "known techniques" applied to the element that makes the invention work. Full blueprints are not required; enough guidance that no inventive faculty is needed is.
- **Why it matters:** Withholding specifics a PHOSITA needs is a fatal § 112(a) flaw and cannot be cured after filing.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a); MPEP 2164.01.

### `F18` Confirm every specific is stated in nonlimiting terms with alternatives
- **Check:** Verify each stated dimension, material, shape, or value is framed as what is presently contemplated for that embodiment, accompanied by alternatives, with ranges stating whether endpoints are included.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Flat declaratives — "The lever is nylon," "the housing is 4 cm." Follow each with a variation clause. "Of my invention" attached to a specific converts an example into a definition.
- **Why it matters:** Specifics stated as absolutes let a court read them into the claims. Specifics stated as examples strengthen enablement without constraining scope.
- **Primary source:** *Phillips*; MPEP 2111.01, 2173.05(c) (ranges).

### `F19` Confirm technical terms are defined and a glossary is supplied when needed
- **Check:** Verify every sophisticated or technical term is defined in plain language, and that a reasonably complex invention carries a glossary with broad definitions.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications; especially computer, biotech, electronic, chemical, medical, and complex mechanical
- **What to look for:** Undefined acronyms, Greek letters, and terms of art. Definitions narrower than ordinary meaning become binding lexicography. Write the glossary after the claims. Place it after the drawing-description section.
- **Why it matters:** Undefined terms invite narrow construction. Over-narrow definitions shrink claim scope.
- **Primary source:** *Phillips*; *Thorner v. Sony*; MPEP 2111.01.

### `F20` Confirm trademarks are replaced by generic terms or properly identified
- **Check:** Verify trademarked products are referred to by generic name where the mark is not needed, and that any mark actually used is capitalized, identified as a mark, used as an adjective with a generic noun, and attributed to its owner.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications; especially chemical and materials
- **What to look for:** Marketplace names that have generic equivalents. The applicant's own new mark for the invention should not appear in the specification.
- **Why it matters:** Defining an element only by a trademark ties enablement to a commercial product whose composition can change.
- **Primary source:** MPEP 608.01(v); 35 U.S.C. § 112(a).

### `F21` Confirm the reader always knows which figure is under discussion
- **Check:** Verify each technical discussion is tied to a named figure, that the text handles roughly one figure at a time, and that any part mentioned but not drawn is expressly flagged as not shown.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Stretches of abstract prose with no "Fig." citation. Off-the-shelf components mentioned in text need a "(not shown)" tag.
- **Why it matters:** Untethered discussion causes examiner objections and narrow readings. Unflagged undrawn parts read as drawing omissions.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.83; MPEP 608.01(g).

## Operation

### `F22` Confirm the Operation section covers every part's function and introduces none
- **Check:** Verify the Operation section describes the working of the whole system and then of every individual part by name and numeral, introduces no part or numeral not already introduced in the description, and stresses advantages without stating limitations.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Numerals that first appear in Operation. Parts described structurally whose function is never explained. Statements of what the invention cannot do.
- **Why it matters:** A specification that describes how to make but not how to use fails § 112(a). Negative statements arm an adversary.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a); MPEP 2164 (how to use).

## Alternative embodiments and ramifications

### `F23` Confirm all important embodiments are described and none is labeled "preferred"
- **Check:** Verify the draft describes, draws, and claims every reasonably important embodiment, each with its own structure-then-operation treatment, and that no embodiment is called "preferred," "main," or "the invention."
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Those three labels. Commercially plausible embodiments mentioned only in passing. Important variants should also appear in the drawings and claims.
- **Why it matters:** Labeling one embodiment preferred invites a court to read the claims onto it. An undisclosed embodiment cannot support broad claims.
- **Primary source:** *Phillips*; 35 U.S.C. § 112(a).

### `F24` Confirm a comprehensive ramifications passage walks every element through every variation
- **Check:** Verify the ramifications passage walks each element and states whether it can be eliminated, duplicated, resized, remade in a different material, shape, or color, connected differently, given a different mode of operation, or made integrally versus separately.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A passage that varies only one or two components. Means-plus-function scope especially depends on the range of structures disclosed.
- **Why it matters:** Courts interpret a single-embodiment patent narrowly. Claim scope, especially under § 112(f), tracks the disclosed structures.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(f); MPEP 2181; *Phillips*.

## Conclusion and Abstract

### `F25` Confirm the Conclusion restates advantages and cedes scope to the claims
- **Check:** Verify a Conclusion, Ramifications, and Scope section exists that restates advantages in embodiment terms, lists remaining alternatives, and states that scope is determined by the claims and their legal equivalents rather than by the illustrated embodiments.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Absence of the section. Absolute language ("completely reliable") instead of comparative. Arguments for nonobviousness do not belong here — state advantages only.
- **Why it matters:** Without an express scope disclaimer, detailed specificities are more easily read as limitations.
- **Primary source:** *Phillips*; MPEP 608.01(d).

### `F26` Confirm the abstract is a single nonlimiting paragraph of 150 words or fewer
- **Check:** Verify the abstract is one paragraph of no more than 150 words on its own page after the claims, free of throat-clearing phrases and of "the invention," with a reference numeral in parentheses after each named part if foreign filing is contemplated.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Word count and paragraph count. "This invention relates to…" Legal words such as "said" and "means." Placement after the claims, heading "Abstract."
- **Why it matters:** Rule 72 requires an abstract. Because it is the most-read part of the document, limiting language there colors every later reading of scope.
- **Primary source:** 37 CFR 1.72; MPEP 608.01(b).

## Language discipline and final review

### `F27` Purge limiting essentiality language and self-restricting statements
- **Check:** Verify the specification contains none of the common limiting words (essential, must, only, the invention, preferred embodiment, and similar), never refers to "the invention" as a defined thing, and contains no negative or hedged statements an adversary could use to narrow or invalidate the patent.
- **Severity:** Serious
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** A literal word search for essentiality and preference language. Speak of embodiments and aspects, in confident but non-absolute terms.
- **Why it matters:** These words invite a judge to limit claim scope to what is called critical or preferred.
- **Primary source:** *SciMed*; *Phillips*; MPEP 2111.01.

### `F28` Enforce writing mechanics and heading discipline
- **Check:** Verify the draft uses short sentences, descriptive headings, plain non-legal language, active voice, a noun before every reference numeral, and clean grammar, spelling, and punctuation.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** Run-on sentences, missing headings, "said" and "means" in the specification or abstract, bare numerals without a preceding noun, passive-voice drift, and unproofread grammar. Legal words belong in the claims, not the description.
- **Why it matters:** Unclear disclosure is interpreted narrowly. Sloppiness costs credibility with the examiner and later readers.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a)–(b); MPEP 608.01.

### `F29` Confirm the draft has been re-read and checked by an independent reader
- **Check:** Verify the draft has been set aside, re-reviewed against completeness and non-limiting standards, and read by a trusted third party who confirmed it teaches how to make and use the invention and does not limit it.
- **Severity:** Quality
- **Applies to:** all applications
- **What to look for:** An identifiable second reader asked to verify enablement, accuracy, clarity, and absence of limiting language. Recent patents in the field are a completeness check; older patents are not the current enablement standard. Signs of a single rushed draft fail.
- **Why it matters:** Enablement and non-limiting defects are cheap to fix before filing and often impossible afterward.
- **Primary source:** 35 U.S.C. § 112(a); 35 U.S.C. § 132 (no new matter).
